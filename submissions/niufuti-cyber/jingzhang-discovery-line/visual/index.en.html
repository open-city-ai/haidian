What does AI actually do here?

Start with the street without AI. Every day, the same kerb has to serve couriers making deliveries, older people crossing the street, and shopkeepers keeping their businesses open. Painted markings, signs, and staff gestures may organize who uses it and for how long, but conflicts still have to be settled on the spot.

AI does one job: turn that conflict into options people can compare, show how each option affects each group, and give the choice back to people. For the kerb, it lays out three options—fixed loading times, anonymous dynamic scheduling, and professional kerbside redesign—and compares detours for older people, extra waiting for couriers, and possible lost orders for shopkeepers. It produces the comparison; it does not decide, and it does not know who you are.

Why so many constraints? A wrong comparison is not just a software error; it can stop a wheelchair user from completing a daily trip. So AI is limited to aggregated rather than personal data. It compares options but does not rank people, and it reports findings without controlling any switch.

Why keep a route that uses no AI at all? A street has to keep working every day. This project is not approved, not authorized, and not implemented, so it may stop. If it stops, deliveries, street crossings, medical visits, and school trips must not stop with it. The order is therefore reversed: first make the everyday route work without AI, and only then add AI on top. If AI goes dark, people return to the route that has remained available all along.
