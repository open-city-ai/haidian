New Propositions of Planning in the AI Era: Eight Fundamental Changes

These eight changes describe not a localized upgrade of planning tools, but a systematic transformation of urban planning — from base data, cognitive method, and workflow to value objectives. AI does more than raise the efficiency of text, drawing, and data processing; more importantly, it changes how planning understands the city, forms judgments, organizes proposals, and continuously enters urban construction and governance.

DimensionTraditional planningChange in the AI era
Data baseSmall-sample static dataFull-domain real-time sensing data (remote sensing, IoT, signaling, transport, energy, feedback)
Cognitive paradigmExperience-drivenJointly driven by data and knowledge; planners judge "which data is trustworthy, which rules apply locally"
Analytical methodSingle analysisMultimodal intelligent analysis, discovering correlations, anomalies, and candidate explanations, then professionally verified
Planning objectPhysical space"People–place–city–system", linking property, population, industry, energy, operations, and social impact
Planning processLinear workflowCollaborative co-creative iteration; new evidence can revise judgments and optimize proposals at any time

These eight changes together show that the essence of AI-native planning is to ground it in urban evidence, support it with intelligent simulation, center it on human–machine joint judgment, and run it through closed-loop iteration — moving planning from a static blueprint toward a dynamic urban decision system.

Three Core Questions: How to Plan · How to Present · How to Apply

The overall positioning of a "world-class AI source" rests on answering three core propositions, which together form a complete logic from method and expression to application.

How to plan: the logic path of AI-native planning

AI-native planning does not append an AI tool to the end of a traditional workflow; rather, from the very start of planning, it organizes the whole process by "readable data, traceable evidence, inferable knowledge, simulatable proposals, explainable decisions, and feedbackable implementation", forming six consecutive stages:

Sensing and data aggregation▶Evidence construction and governance▶Knowledge graph and reasoning▶Proposal generation and simulation▶Decision support and coordination▶Monitoring, evaluation and iteration

The entire logic path embodies four core traits: data continuity, knowledge-driven, AI-empowered, closed-loop iteration — planning no longer ends with deliverable review, but forms a continuous operating process of "sensing — evidence — reasoning — generation — decision — implementation — feedback".

How to present planning: a new content-interface expression

Planning presentation in the AI era should not merely produce an atlas or a 3D model, but establish a unified planning master, then form six categories of information interfaces according to different users' tasks and cognition: decision interface (conclusion · evidence · risk · action), professional-technical interface (data · method · model · recheck), spatial-experience interface (2D/3D/scenario/viewpoint), approval-rules interface (metrics · conditions · exemptions), project-investment interface (list · schedule · capital · responsibility), and public-and-operations interface (change · progress · feedback · operation).

Planning presentation should have four traits: precise expression, layered presentation, visual interaction, traceable explanation — no longer "making people understand the proposal", but letting different actors make decisions, review, build, operate, and give feedback around one planning master.

How to apply planning: new scenarios and applications in the AI era

AI-driven planning application follows a common path — problem identification — proposal generation — countermeasure simulation — scenario design — execution — feedback optimization: organizing real urban problems into a continuous planning system grounded in trusted evidence, methodized through multi-proposal simulation, anchored by spatial-operational integration, and mechanized through open collaboration and version iteration, so that the Jingzhang Railway urban design shifts from a one-off deliverable into an urban action process that can keep learning, validating, and updating.

Building the Base: From Data Repository to Urban Spatio-Temporal Data

Build a "full-factor, multi-scale, long-cycle" data foundation for the Jingzhang Railway Innovation Belt, and elevate multi-source data into a computable, inferable, traceable knowledge network to underpin problem identification and proposal simulation.

An urban evidence system does not centrally store all urban data, but transforms dispersed, heterogeneous, variable-quality, and differently-permissioned data — through governance, verification, and interpretation — into trusted evidence capable of supporting planning judgments. Its core logic is: multi-source data intake — unified resource management — data governance — evidence formation — planning application. Only when data's source, time, spatial extent, processing method, quality level, and applicability boundary are made explicit can it truly enter planning judgment.

2.1 Data Foundation

Static spatial evidence

  • Land use, buildings, roads, facilities, terrain, water systems
  • Property rights, ecological sensitive areas, historical and cultural resources
  • 3D urban form, skyline, public-space quality

Dynamic operational evidence

  • Block-level activity and traffic flow from public statistics, onsite counts or lawfully authorized aggregates; no assumed access to mobile signalling
  • Energy consumption, water consumption, visitor stay, facility usage
  • Meteorological and environmental monitoring

Institutional and project evidence

  • Policy documents, approval conditions, fiscal budgets, land supply
  • Construction projects, operating rights, contractual arrangements
  • Implementation entities and funding sources

Social cognition evidence

  • Resident opinions, enterprise demands, online discussions, complaints and suggestions
  • Local knowledge, expert judgment
  • Stakeholder interviews

2.2 Data Graph

An urban evidence graph is not drawing urban data as a relationship network, but — grounded in urban objects — organizing "where data comes from, what it demonstrates, which judgments it supports or refutes, what decisions it forms, and what results it ultimately produces" into a queryable, verifiable, traceable, updatable spatio-temporal relationship system.

Subject base

  • Spatial units (parcels, blocks, corridors, nodes)
  • Functional facilities (transport, industry, public services, computing facilities)
  • Property-right entities and governance entities

Structural relations

  • Spatial relations (adjacency, containment, reachability, visibility)
  • Functional relations (synergy, conflict, dependency, substitution)
  • Temporal relations (evolution, renewal, project sequencing)

Professional thematic graphs

  • Transport–land–industry interaction graph
  • Heritage conservation–urban renewal trade-off graph
  • S&T resource–supporting facility matching graph
  • Computing facility–safety resilience assessment graph

Other relations

  • Policy–project–space mapping relations
  • Public demand–planning response relations

Above the "single map" and CIM, the evidence graph adds semantic association, evidence provenance, planning reasoning, decision basis, and implementation feedback, going beyond an ordinary knowledge graph with four key layers: source and credibility; support and refutation relations; reasoning and decision processes; action outcomes and their feedback.

Methodological Shift: From Correlation Identification to Testable Mechanism Hypotheses

This layer is the key step entering from "problem identification" in the overall six-step progressive logic, and its methodological shift can be summarized in one sentence:

Shift from correlations to testable mechanism hypotheses. At this stage, the proposal states candidate mechanisms and indicators only. A real pilot must preregister treatment and comparison groups, confounders and identification assumptions, and report uncertainty and robustness checks before making causal claims.

Traditional problem identification mostly stays at the "symptom" and "correlation" level, easily mistaking co-occurrence for causation and thus proposing remedies that treat symptoms but not root causes. This chapter makes three methodological turns:

These mechanisms are testable hypotheses, not established causal conclusions.

3.4.2 Symptom 1: Head-On Conflict Between Demand Surge and Siting Constraints

A compute center is not an ordinary industrial building; it has strict rigid requirements for siting conditions, while the spatial reality of high-density built-up areas conflicts with them systematically:

  • Energy constraint: power supply must be ample and reliable, communications fast and unobstructed, and transport convenient — yet the core area of the innovation corridor has limited grid capacity and substation increments, with large-scale compute electricity competing against urban residential electricity for the same grid;
  • Environmental constraint: the natural environment must be clean and the ambient temperature conducive to energy saving — yet high-density built-up areas have a pronounced heat-island effect and complex sources of dust and oil smoke, compressing year-round natural-cooling hours and passively raising PUE;
  • Hazard avoidance: must stay away from dust, oil smoke, harmful gases, and corrosive, flammable, or explosive materials — yet fuel and gas stations, warehousing and logistics, and cooking fumes are scattered throughout the built-up area, making safe distances hard to satisfy item by item;
  • Disaster avoidance: must stay away from flood, earthquake, and other natural-disaster hazard areas — yet riverside/waterfront segments, rising-groundwater zones, and fault-zone influence ranges along the corridor all carry siting boundary risks;
  • Functional compatibility: medium- and large-scale compute centers should not be built within residential and commercial areas — yet "compute-on-the-street" pushes compute nodes precisely toward high-density residential and commercial mixed areas, with noise, heat dissipation, and transport directly adjacent to residents' lives.

3.4.3 Symptom 2: Concentrated Layout and Disaster-Risk Exposure

Compute facilities naturally tend toward "concentrated layout, shared facilities" to amortize costs, but against the backdrop of frequent disasters and normalized extreme weather, concentrated layout is compressing systemic risk into single points:

  • Flood risk: if low-lying, waterlogging-prone locations such as riverside/ditch-side areas, depressed bridge zones, and rising-groundwater zones carry compute functions, they risk backflow, submergence, and total shutdown under rainstorms and super-standard floods; if first-floor key equipment sits below the 100-year flood level, one extreme rainfall can cause catastrophic loss;
  • Seismic risk: if compute centers' seismic-fortification category and structural system follow ordinary Class-C buildings, the probability of cabinet overturning, pipeline rupture, and cluster paralysis rises markedly under strong earthquakes;
  • Environmental risk: compute nodes near strong-vibration sources (rail transit, large machinery) and strong-electromagnetic-field sources (substations, high-voltage corridors) face sustained pressure on equipment stability and data integrity;
  • Single-point risk: compute clusters concentrate in an "island" fashion; once the host area suffers power outage, fire, or waterlogging, clusters lacking cross-node takeover and off-site disaster recovery face total failure, with compute interruption directly transmitted to the urban operations and industrial innovation depending on it.

3.4.4 Symptom 3: Absent Spatial-Resilience Design

Even after siting lands, the spatial-resilience design of compute facilities themselves still has four pervasive gaps, leaving "disaster resistance" and "reconfigurability" on paper:

① Flood-resistance gap. Key data, governance control, and core compute are placed in flood-prone underground space or low-lying first floors; building entrances, ramps, vents, and utility openings lack backflow protection; underground-garage ramps and utility entries are not zone-isolated — under extreme rainfall, the rainwater-backflow path points directly at core equipment.

② Fire-resistance gap. Computer-room, power, and energy-storage areas are not physically separated, so one fire can spread to the entire compute cluster; the park's ring fire lane, independent fire-compartment isolation, and fire/emergency circulation are insufficient; key systems such as power supply and cooling lack the "one fault does not propagate" compartment design.

③ Phasing gap. The master plan is not planned once with reserved long-term expansion land; computer rooms, power distribution, and cooling trunk networks are not built once; compute units are not cluster-laid-out, causing the "one-step" full build-out to detach from the real business-growth rhythm, both raising investment risk and forcing later expansion to disturb already-online business.

④ Reconfigurability gap. Civil work and hardware are deeply coupled, so cabinet density cannot accommodate evolution from low-power servers to ultra-high-density liquid-cooled full cabinets; power-distribution and cooling modules cannot stack-expand; utilities, trays, and interfaces are unstandardized, so hardware replacement or architecture adjustment requires large-scale civil rework; a single computer room's or cluster's fault cannot be isolated, propagating along shared systems.

Figure 3 · Comparison of spatial-resilience gaps and the ideal framework. A systematic gap exists between the current four-dimension gaps (red) and the ideal resilience profile (green) of "elevated critical layers + compartmental isolation + phased growth + modular decoupling".

3.4.5 Symptom 4: Insufficient Factor and Institutional Resilience

A compute center's resilience depends not only on the computer room itself, but more on factor supply and operating institutions. There are currently obvious "soft-resilience" shortcomings at both the factor and institutional levels:

Factor-resilience shortcomings

  • Human single-point dependency: operation capability over-depends on a few local key personnel, lacking off-site expert-support channels, AB-role rotation, and knowledge-base accumulation, so loss of key personnel becomes a system risk;
  • Vendor lock-in risk: compute supply, hardware, software, and scheduling platforms are bound to a single supplier; failure or supply cutoff of a single chip, cloud platform, or scheduling system is directly transmitted to the entire cluster;
  • Open-source supply-chain risk: if base scheduling frameworks, cluster management, and monitoring components directly use overseas open-source versions without domestic adaptation-hardening or internal maintenance branches, upstream discontinuation or vulnerability outbreaks leave no backstop;
  • Data-disaster-recovery gap: hot/warm/cold data is not stored in graded multi-copy form, compute and disaster-recovery nodes are not physically dispersed across risk zones, and training tasks lack checkpoint-resume mechanisms, so hardware damage zeroes out tasks;
  • Full-configuration hard binding of compute resources: general, intelligent, and super-computing resources are not pooled, with no degraded-operation mode, so under extreme faults core compute cannot be retained and non-core business cannot be shut down, and there is no cross-regional scheduling channel when local compute is insufficient.

Institutional-resilience shortcomings

  • Build-heavy, operate-light: resilience indicators (acceptable degradation ratio, RTO/RPO, fault-handling flow, chaos-drill requirements) are not embedded in the whole project feasibility, design, acceptance, and operation-assessment flow, looking only at PUE and compute peak;
  • Standard-system confusion: the two systems of infrastructure grade (computer-room construction standard) and compute-business grade (intelligent-computing capability grading) are confused, with interfaces, hardware, and scheduling protocols using private closed standards, leaving insufficient room for future new standards and new hardware iteration;
  • Missing governance coordination: emergency-linkage responsibilities and response flows among compute operators, grid companies, telecom operators, tenants, and regulators are unclear, with agent-alarm information siloed and lacking a unified event bus and global visibility.

3.4.6 candidate mechanism chain: From Demand Surge to Resilience Deficit

Stringing the symptoms above together, this problem's mechanism hypothesis structure presents a clear progressive chain:

Compute-demand surge (large-model training / agent inference / hundred-thousand-card clusters) → Construction wave surges toward high-density built-up areas (nearby compute, near talent, near users) → Siting constraints yield to cost and pace (energy / disaster / functional compatibility compressed) → Concentrated layout + exposure to low-lying / hazard-source / strong-vibration areas → Spatial resilience simplified (flood/fire-resistance gaps / full build-out at once / non-reconfigurable) → Factor and institutional resilience missing (single-point dependency / build-heavy operate-light) → Overall shutdown risk under extreme events
Final outcome: accumulated layout risk + resilience deficit → compute supply interruption → transmitted to AI industry and urban operations

The key leverage points lie in two intervenable links: "safety-boundary front-loading" at the siting end (treating disaster avoidance, hazard spacing, and functional compatibility as non-negotiable rigid constraints, with veto power in siting comparison) and "resilience-metric embedding" at the design-operation end (writing redundancy, degradation, reconfiguration, and self-healing into feasibility, design, acceptance, and operational assessment). The former decides whether risk enters; the latter decides whether risk can be absorbed.

3.5.2 Symptom 1: the Spatial Contest Between Conservation and Renewal Demands

Conservation and renewal raise mutually pulling spatial demands on the same corridor, and both are regarded as "non-negotiable":

  • Conservation end: demands authenticity conservation — completely preserving rails, road bridges, welded components, and other original industrial relics to ensure the authenticity and legibility of historical information; demands integrity conservation — extending the conservation scope from individual relics to the linear corridor and its historical nodes, covering roadbeds, stations, workshops, bridges, and all other elements; demands adaptive-reuse conservation — converting static relics into living landscapes within public space, making historical elements participatory and experiential;
  • Renewal end: demands stitching the city — breaking the long-term severance of urban space by railways and elevated facilities, opening dead-end roads, and reconstructing the severed urban fabric; demands livelihood priority — precisely responding to the daily-life needs of diverse groups such as surrounding residents, commuters, and university students and staff; demands ecological intensification — converting idle "grey infrastructure" into green public space.

On the surface the two are complementary, but in reality they contend for the same corridor land: the space where conservation demands "preserve as-is" and the space where renewal demands "function implantation" are often the same roadbed, the same station house, the same under-bridge space. When "conservation first" is read as "freeze as-is", renewal corridors, facilities, and industrial functions have nowhere to go; when "stitch the city" is prioritized, the authenticity of original relics faces the risk of being cut, wrapped, or obscured.

Figure 1 · Spatial-contest diagram. Conservation (green) demands "preserve as-is" while renewal (red) demands "function implantation"; the two contest the same corridor space head-on, lacking a reconciliation framework of grading, zoning, and sequencing.

3.5.3 Symptom 2: Structural Contradiction Between Point Conservation and the Linear Corridor

The heritage attribute of the Jingzhang Railway is "linear", yet in reality conservation and renewal mostly unfold in "points", producing a mismatch in structure and scale:

  • Heritage points acting independently: station houses, bridges, tunnel mouths, turnouts, and other heritage nodes are often designated and protected point by point, lacking a unified corridor-wide heritage-value genealogy and display framework, so the historical narrative is chopped into isolated fragments;
  • Broken corridor-heritage continuity: the 9-km linear corridor presents completely different states in different segments — some sections already built into parks, some still enclosed walls, some occupied by temporary parking and low-efficiency functions — the heritage is spatially intermittent and cannot form an overall pattern of "one belt stringing together multiple activated points";
  • Misaligned conservation and renewal scales: conservation takes the "point" as unit, renewal the "parcel", and operation the "segment" — the three boundaries are inconsistent, making it hard to align conservation plans, urban-renewal proposals, and operational management spatially;
  • Missing value interpretation: heritage value interpretation stops at "what has been preserved", lacking a systematic expression of "why it matters and how it is experienced", with no perceivable narrative connection between industrial relics and contemporary urban life.

3.5.4 Symptom 3: the Two Polar Risks of Static Freezing and Renewal-Driven Destruction

In the tug-of-war between conservation and renewal, practice often slides toward two extremes, while the truly needed "conservation-through-use" middle path is chronically absent:

Extreme 1: static freezing. Reducing "conservation first" to "seal as-is", heritage is fenced off and removed from use, becoming a "dead specimen" to be viewed from afar but not used up close. Its consequences: relics are isolated from daily life, cultural value cannot be perceived, and conservation becomes a fiscal burden; spaces lacking functional support rapidly decay, generating new safety and environmental problems; conservation investment can hardly form a sustainable operational loop.

Extreme 2: renewal-driven destruction. Reducing "stitch the city" to "tear down and rebuild", original relics are demolished, cut, or wrapped in the name of renewal, or large-scale new functions are implanted without heritage assessment. Its consequences: the authenticity and legibility of historical information are damaged, and the memory carrier of the railway disappears; once destruction occurs it is irreversible, and heritage fracture becomes a fait accompli; public trust is damaged, making it harder to build social consensus for subsequent conservation and renewal.

What is lacking between the two poles is precisely the "conservation-through-use" middle path: letting heritage continue through use via reasonable function implantation, and endowing industrial relics with contemporary vitality through functional substitution rather than functional freezing — old waiting facilities converted into resting benches, industrial gantry cranes turned into iconic check-in spots, abandoned carriages converted into cultural display or light-dining spaces. The absence of this path keeps conservation and renewal locked in an either/or opposition.

Figure 2 · Two-polar-risk diagram. Static freezing (left) and renewal-driven destruction (right) are the two failure exits of the conservation–renewal tug-of-war; the middle path of "conservation through use" (dashed box) is precisely what is missing.

3.5.5 Symptom 4: Dispersed Multiple Actors and Insufficient Public Participation

The Jingzhang corridor spans multiple sub-districts and multiple property-right entities, with highly dispersed conservation and renewal responsibilities and interests, while coordination mechanisms and public participation are chronically absent:

  • Dispersed actors: the railway property owner, corridor units, street communities, merchants, and residents each manage their own segment, lacking a unified responsibility boundary and coordination platform, producing a situation of "no one leading conservation, renewal going its own way, and no one responsible for operation";
  • Unequal interests: the responsibility of conservation (restricting construction, maintaining relics) often falls on local actors, while the benefits of renewal (land appreciation, foot-traffic import) are shared by a larger scope; the mismatch of responsibility, rights, and benefits weakens the initiative of conservation actors;
  • Insufficient public participation: residents, students and staff, and local enterprises — as daily users of the heritage — participate insufficiently in key links such as heritage-list designation, renewal-option comparison, and operational-content planning, so cultural identification can hardly convert into co-building and co-governance action;
  • Lack of sustainable operation: after public spaces are built, there is a lack of routine cultural-content operation — railway-themed exhibitions, cultural-creative markets, historical and cultural tours are missing, so the spaces decay again from management absence, forming a "built-then-idle" cycle.

3.5.6 candidate mechanism chain: From Conservation Rigidity to Heritage Fracture

Stringing the symptoms above together, the mechanism hypothesis structure of this problem presents two interlocking paths:

High heritage-value recognition (first self-built trunk railway) → Conservation boundaries tend toward rigid control → Renewal space constrained, activation path fuzzy (unclear what and how to change) → Weak renewal momentum → corridor keeps fragmenting, relics isolated → Insufficient public participation → declining cultural identification → Conservation and renewal fall into an either/or deadlock
Final outcome: heritage fracture — a triple fracture of historical-memory continuity, spatial-fabric continuity, and social-identification continuity

The key leverage points lie in two intervenable links: the "graded-control rules" at the conservation end (limiting rigid control to the conservation core area and reserving elasticity in the buffer coordination area, achieving "rigid-elastic combination, one-policy-per-object") and the "conservation-through-use mechanism" at the activation end (letting heritage continue through use via function implantation, cultural operation, and public co-building). The former solves "over-rigid conservation", the latter solves "over-aggressive change", jointly converging the two polar risks onto the middle path.

4.2.1 Strategy Positioning: Turning the Spatial Stock into an Innovation-Operations Traffic Entry

Along the Jingzhang Railway Innovation Belt lie large amounts of low-efficiency space precipitated by railway rerouting, industrial upgrading, and complex property rights: grey spaces under bridges, idle station houses, old industrial courtyards, corner green spaces, and office ground-floor retail vacant at night. These are not "negative assets" of urban development, but "dormant assets" not yet precisely matched to suitable projects and operators.

This strategy borrows the "stock-asset renewal cloud" concept — fit projects to spaces, tailor spaces to projects — and understands fine-grained activation as a lightweight operating system running through 4.1–4.3: it does not rely on large demolition and construction, but rather, through spatial-asset identification, AI-assisted decision, precise spatial activation, intelligent operation scheduling, and feedback optimization, turns low-efficiency space into a low-cost, high-elasticity entry for innovation facilities and life services. In other words, what 4.3 answers is "how the innovation belt's space sustains itself and keeps generating value through operation", not "how many more square meters of new buildings to construct".

The mini-program homepage sets four entries — "Resource Plaza, Resource Map, Policy Information, Forum" — corresponding respectively to asset discovery, spatial positioning, rule query, and community co-governance, serving as the lightweight interface through which the Jingzhang corridor's low-efficiency space moves from "dormant assets" to "dynamic operation".

4.2.3 AI-Assisted Decision: Acupuncture-Point Identification, Proposal Generation, and Actor Matching

On the basis of asset digitization, AI plays the assisting decision role of "diagnose—match—generate", converting fuzzy spatial problems into actionable project lists.

Acupuncture-point identification logic

AI acupuncture-point identification follows a five-step flow of "data fusion → candidate screening → comprehensive scoring → priority ranking → field verification":

  1. Conditional data fusion: prioritize public asset records, POI, planning conditions, onsite counts and de-identified complaint statistics; do not assume access to mobile-signalling, Wi-Fi-probe or personal social-account data; any additional source requires legality, necessity and DPIA review;
  2. Candidate screening: set hard thresholds — relatively clear property or coordination possibility, no major structural-safety hazards, basic accessibility;
  3. Comprehensive scoring: compute each point's composite score based on vitality-evaluation indicators, and identify weak links (e.g., large facility gap but high foot traffic);
  4. Priority ranking: combine implementation difficulty, demonstration effect, investment scale, and stakeholder support to generate an annual acupuncture-project library;
  5. Field verification: carry out rapid survey and community interviews at high-priority points, correcting "data blind spots" in AI judgment.

Spatial digital profile

Generate a dynamic "spatial digital profile" for each asset, as the unified language for matching and operational decisions:

  • Basic profile: location, scale, tenure, current function, constraints;
  • Vitality profile: foot-traffic time series, crowd composition, activity types, peaks and troughs;
  • Demand profile: population, industry, and facility gaps within the surrounding 5/10-minute walking circle;
  • Opportunity profile: suitable formats, potential operators, expected revenue range, stackable 4.3/4.4/4.5 strategies;
  • Risk profile: structural hazards, property disputes, policy restrictions, NIMBY risk, municipal bottlenecks.

Space–project–operator matching mechanism

Establish a three-party two-way matching model, unifying "space seeks project" and "project seeks space" into one algorithmic framework:

  • Space-side input: digital profile, constraints, owner intent, revenue expectation, acceptable operation modes (lease/joint-operation/revenue-share/public-benefit);
  • Project-side input: business plan, target customer, investment scale, operating experience, social benefit, time-shared use demand;
  • Operator-side input: entity type and authorized project-experience and capability tags; do not create personal credit scores or use unrelated behavioral history or geographic preferences;
  • Matching algorithm: generate recommended ranking based on multi-objective optimization (spatial compatibility, expected revenue, social value, risk controllability, operational sustainability);
  • Decision closed loop: recommendations enter the "asset details—I'll operate" application channel, the property owner reviews online, and the platform continuously tracks fulfillment performance and feeds back to the model.

Micro-renewal proposal generation flow

For the screened acupuncture points, AI assists in generating "light, fast, economical" micro-renewal proposals:

  1. Problem diagnosis: identify the point's weak links (lack of seating, lighting, greenery, function, barrier-free access);
  2. User profile: analyze the spatio-temporal distribution and needs of surrounding groups (commuters, residents, students, visitors);
  3. Toolkit matching: combine compatible modules from the standardized toolkit (paving, furniture, pavilions, greenery, lighting, digital facilities);
  4. Scenario packaging: overlay localized functions (café-book bar, community kitchen, shared meeting room, courier station, cycling service station, pet-friendly facilities);
  5. Cost estimation: output a rough budget range based on module unit prices and workload;
  6. Proposal output: generate floor-plan schematic, bill of materials, implementation schedule, operation-mode suggestions, and expected effects;
  7. Public feedback: publicize the proposal on the online platform; residents like, comment, and vote; AI aggregates opinions and assists optimization.

4.2.4 Precise Spatial Activation: Micro-Renewal Toolkit, Innovation Boxes, and Community Embedding

AI decisions ultimately land in concrete spatial renovation and function embedding. This stage emphasizes "small cuts, quick results, accumulable", activating dormant assets with low-cost acupuncture-style intervention.

Standardized micro-renewal toolkit

Build a replicable, low-cost renewal "menu", quickly combining according to a point's weak links:

  • Ground and interface: permeable-paving refurbishment, railway sleeper-rail texture paving, movable platforms, modular benches, vertical green walls;
  • Facilities and services: movable furniture, modular pavilions, public Wi-Fi, charging piles, smart light poles, courier lockers, drinking water;
  • Ecology and landscape: flower boxes, rain gardens, shade canopies, pet-friendly facilities, bike racks;
  • Digital and interaction: information screens, QR-code guides, NFC tags, environmental-monitoring sensors, foot-traffic counters.

Localized scenario implantation

Embed differentiated functions according to the characteristics of the surrounding population, avoiding "a thousand points all alike":

  • Commuting nodes: café-book bar, quick breakfast, temporary office, cycling service station;
  • Residential communities: community kitchen, shared meeting room, courier station, childcare waiting space, pet-friendly corner;
  • Around universities: open-source lounge, joint discussion room, impromptu roadshow corner, 24-hour study pod;
  • Cultural-tourism nodes: railway-themed display, cultural-creative retail, light dining, AR check-in spots.

Innovation boxes and community-space network

Within the innovation belt, lay out small community spaces such as "innovation boxes", "joint discussion rooms", and "open-source lounges" at a 5–10 minute walking scale:

  • Functional positioning: support chance-encounter exchange, impromptu roadshows, cross-sector cooperation, and small events;
  • Spatial form: modularly assembled, movable, time-shared and reusable, 20–100 m²;
  • Service embedding: integrate life facilities such as short-rent apartments, shared kitchens, laundry & fitness, childcare, and pet care around community spaces, reducing innovation talent's life friction;
  • Enterprise–community sharing interface: encourage enterprises to open meeting rooms, exhibition halls, green spaces, and parking resources to the community, in exchange for public-service points or brand exposure, forming a reciprocal mechanism.

AI time-shared operation scheduling mechanism

  • Slot granularity: build a year-round bookable calendar in 1-hour or 30-minute units;
  • User stratification: distinguish resident daily, enterprise service, community activity, commercial operation, maintenance & cleaning, with different priorities and pricing rules;
  • Dynamic pricing: peak-hour market price, off-peak member price, public-benefit hours low-cost or free;
  • Conflict resolution: when multiple parties apply for the same slot, auto-adjudicate by priority "safety maintenance > contracted long-term lease > public-benefit activity > commercial activity > temporary application";
  • Smart booking: users view real-time available slots online, book with one click, pay, and obtain access/pass codes;
  • Handover management: clarify facility handover, cleaning, and security responsibilities across time slots, lowering multi-party shared-use transaction costs.

Function-mixing admission and agreement templates

  • Admission list: formulate catalogs of encouraged, restricted, and prohibited functions, allowing moderate mixing of commercial, cultural, and innovation services while ensuring safety and compliance;
  • Agreement template: clarify the using entity, time slot, fee standard, facility handover, safety management, insurance, and breach liability, forming a standardized time-shared operation agreement;
  • Light-asset matchmaking: introduce professional operators, community curators, university entrepreneurship teams, and other actors, lowering the burden on government and property owners through short-lease, joint-operation, and revenue-share models.

4.2.6 Feedback Optimization: Community-Operation Agent and Co-Governance Platform

The last link of the stock-asset renewal cloud loop is continuously collecting usage feedback, making community self-organization the sustained driver of spatial operation.

Community-operation Agent

Deploy an intelligent community-operation Agent as the "connector" among residents, operators, and managers:

  • Demand sensing: automatically analyze forum posts, demand releases, voting data, and activity feedback, identifying high-frequency needs (e.g., "need nighttime fitness space", "hope to add childcare waiting area");
  • Spatial matching: based on demand type, crowd size, and time-slot preference, automatically recommend suitable acupuncture points or community spaces and generate activity-plan drafts;
  • Resource matchmaking: match activities with potential curators, volunteers, partner merchants, and policy support;
  • Smart reminders: push activity notifications to target audiences and remind operators of facility maintenance and handover;
  • Feedback learning: after activities, automatically collect satisfaction, participation, energy use, and safety events, updating spatial profiles and operation strategies;
  • Point incentives: record residents' contributions to co-creation, volunteering, and activity organization, redeemable for public-space use rights or cultural-creative benefits.

Demand release and forum co-governance

  • Demand release: users fill in demand title, description, type, location, and site photos, releasing demands for rent-seeking, site-seeking, renovation, cooperation, etc.;
  • AI matching: the system automatically matches citizen/enterprise demands with the platform's online assets, and reverse-pushes potential operation opportunities to property owners;
  • Forum discussion: discuss specific assets or renewal topics; residents, designers, and operators co-build proposals, like and comment, forming a transparent co-governance atmosphere;
  • Policy information: centrally display policies on stock-asset revitalization, urban renewal, park supporting services, lowering the information threshold.

Performance feedback and model iteration

  • Operation-performance dashboard: display each asset's usage rate, revenue, satisfaction, energy use, and safety events in real time;
  • Problem closed loop: establish ticket flow for complaints, faults, and misuse, tracking resolution;
  • Model iteration: feed operation data back into the asset database and matching algorithm, continuously optimizing acupuncture-point identification, proposal generation, and actor-matching performance.

4.3.2.1 Current road network

The roads in this belt form a "ring + vertical axis" skeleton. Three ring roads (North 3rd/4th/5th Ring) and the Jingzang Expressway carry express traffic; Zhongguancun Avenue, Xueyuan Road, and others carry north–south arterial traffic; Zhichun Road, Chengfu Road, and Qinghua East Road carry east–west connections. The Jingzhang corridor was originally a railway; after its ground conversion into a heritage park, road micro-circulation and branch-road networks on both sides are generally insufficient, with many dead-end roads — the main shortcoming for slow traffic and parcel accessibility.

4.4.1 Strategy Positioning: Flexible Resilience Is the Essential Definition of a World-Class AI Source

This strategy does not interpret "resilience" as whether a single computer room can withstand disasters, nor does it take "how many cards and redundant lines to build" as its goal; instead it proposes a more fundamental judgment: the core competitiveness of a world-class AI source is precisely its ability to keep producing new ideas, new enterprises, and new standards amid sustained turbulence in technology paradigms, supply chains, and talent and capital cycles.

This definition converges "flexible resilience" and "world-class AI source" from two parallel matters into one: what distinguishes a source from an ordinary AI park is not denser factors but stronger adaptive capacity — whether the ecosystem can self-substitute, self-reorganize, and self-regenerate when the model paradigm shifts from CNN to Transformer and then to the next paradigm, when compute supply is hit externally, when capital enters a down cycle, or when talent temporarily flows out.

In other words, what 4.4 answers is "how a world-class AI source keeps self-substituting, self-reorganizing, and self-regenerating amid sustained turbulence in technology paradigms, supply chains, and talent and capital cycles", not "how many cards, redundant lines, or square meters of computer rooms to build". 4.4.3 Three Core Strategies

This strategy lands the "adaptive capacity of a world-class AI source" on three executable core strategies, corresponding respectively to the elastic layout of the compute base, the systemic coordination of energy–compute–urban safety, and headroom compatibility for future technology evolution, forming the landing backbone of "factor resilience—spatial resilience—institutional resilience".

Core Strategy 1: elastic layout and redundancy design of the compute base (three-tier compute network)

As the base-type factor, compute is organized through the "peripheral training—Zhongzhiyuan full-stack inference—Origin nearby light compute—Dazhongsi edge" three-tier compute network, so that compute infrastructure is neither rejected by high-density built-up areas nor fails to truly serve as the source's enabling base:

Core Strategy 2: coordinated planning of energy–compute–urban safety

As the base-type factor, compute's energy supply, energy-use efficiency, and waste-heat utilization are not "secondary issues", but the first-principles constraint of source resilience. This strategy plans energy, compute, and urban safety as one coordinated whole:

  • Energy-supply resilience: compute clusters adopt multi-source power of "green power first + energy-storage regulation + dual-circuit supply + emergency diesel generator", with key inference and governance control not relying on a single power source; power is reserved for future compute-density increases;
  • Energy-use efficiency: nearby-light-compute centers adopt liquid/high-density cooling, benchmarking PUE≤1.25; prioritize free cooling and natural cold sources, reducing energy use and urban heat island;
  • Waste-heat recovery (formal coordinated design): compute waste heat serves as a controllable heat source, prioritizing building domestic hot water and winter heating, coordinating with stable nearby heat users (communities, public buildings), and incorporated into the smart dual-carbon platform's energy–carbon sub-account accounting, turning "waste heat" from passive emission into a measurable low-carbon resource;
  • Compute–grid coordination: compute load participates in peak-shaving, valley-filling, and demand response, actively shedding load as an interruptible load during grid peaks or extreme weather in exchange for the power safety of urban public services;
  • Urban-safety coordination: compute clusters link with Scenario 4's water-safety "four-forecasting", pre-scheduling key tasks to the flood-safe Zhongzhiyuan/periphery during rainstorm warnings, with local nearby light compute entering load-limit/emergency states, preventing compute infrastructure from becoming a fragile point of waterlogging, fire, or power accidents.

The above coordination is a conceptual proposal; specific power capacity, PUE, waste-heat scale, and connection methods must be specially argued after obtaining official power and municipal materials; the shared hall can display recovered-heat destinations and measured energy use, but must not write unimplemented waste-heat utilization as a definite commitment.

Core Strategy 3: headroom and compatibility for future technology evolution

Compute paradigms iterate extremely fast; this strategy reserves "upgradable, replaceable, non-locked" physical and institutional space for the base:

  • Heterogeneous compatibility: power distribution, cabinets, and network reserve interfaces for GPU / domestic ASIC / FPGA / future architectures, avoiding lock-in to a single chip route;
  • Cooling compatibility: nearby-light-compute centers reserve air-cooling–liquid-cooling switching capability, with cooling systems designed for scalability;
  • Power and network reservation: reserve power capacity and fiber ducts by future compute density, leaving physical space for optical interconnect, compute-in-storage, and other directions;
  • Modular prefabrication: compute clusters use modular prefabricated rooms, expandable in phases and replaceable as a whole;
  • Institutional headroom: compute-scheduling rules, energy quotas, and governance mechanisms iterate in versions, avoiding premature fixation that locks in technology evolution.

The following three scenarios elevate source resilience from "in-park principles" to "urban operations": water-safety "four-forecasting" demonstrates how AI-native inference supports spatial resilience under extreme weather, resilience switching demonstrates state-differentiated response under turbulent shocks, and the dual-carbon platform demonstrates the measurable operation of the ecological base.

Scenario 4: real-time urban-operations inference — water-safety "four-forecasting"

Positioning: this is the key scenario landing the "resilient source" from abstract principle to real urban operations, using water safety as the entry point to show how AI-native inference supports spatial resilience under extreme weather and urban waterlogging. The Qinghe, Xiaoyue River, and Zhuan River are built-up-area water systems, structurally isomorphic to Ningbo's Yong River "basin + urban waterlogging + underground space + lifeline" problem, with direct migration conditions.

AI-native architecture (multi-agent "four-forecasting" closed loop): organized by the competition's "AI-native, multi-agent collaboration" requirement, not traditional water-affairs informatization:

  1. Sensing Agent (monitoring): aggregate the "space-ground-waterworks" sensing network — rainfall, river level, gates, pump stations, pipe networks, video, satellites, drones, and ground inspection robots, uploading structured information;
  2. Forecast Agent (model): meteorological—hydrological—hydrodynamic coupling, forecasting water levels and submergence risk in advance;
  3. Warning Agent (threshold/knowledge): threshold triggering and expert-knowledge analysis, automatically generating risk lists and pushing to authorized managers;
  4. Preview Agent (simulation): 2D-3D coupled flood-submergence model, simulating the consequences of "how a gate is scheduled" and "what if a pipe segment fails";
  5. Contingency Agent (scheduling): linking drainage scheduling, pump stations, gates, and cross-node takeover suggestions;
  6. Review Agent (iteration): post-event comparison of forecast vs. actual, iterating model parameters.

Human review and privacy boundary: all drainage, gate, evacuation, fire, and public-safety decisions must be confirmed by humans, with AI only outputting suggestions; only structured monitoring data and desensitized indicators are processed, no personal trajectories collected, no personal profiles output.

Operating entities: Zhongzhiyuan urban reasoning center + urban-operations department + digital-technology support (capability reference at the conceptual stage; specific service relations pending formal argumentation).

Scenario 5: resilience switching under turbulence and shocks

Resilience switching covers the three layers of "factor—space—institution", triggered by observable indicators and connected with Scenario 4's water-safety "four-forecasting":

  • Attention state (trigger: key-factor indicators deviate from normal or water-safety warning escalates): raise monitoring frequency of key factors — talent loss, enterprise relocation, data risk, compute supply, water-level leakage, power and structural status;
  • Load-limit state (trigger: local shock, need to preserve core): relocate or downgrade non-critical innovation activities, ensuring the continuity of core R&D, trusted data circulation, and urban public services;
  • Emergency state (trigger: extreme weather / waterlogging / power or structural alarm): isolate faulty zones (physical or data partitioning), cross-node takeover of key business (Zhongzhiyuan/peripheral compute takeover, off-site data-copy activation), safe shutdown of key functions;
  • Recovery state (trigger: shock removed): check power, structure, cooling, data integrity, and talent/enterprise return, then recover in phases.

The water-safety "four-forecasting" forecast—warning—preview—contingency is exactly one of the decision bases for the "attention→load-limit→emergency" switching. This process is displayed in the exhibition hall in a simplified, desensitized way, making "source resilience" understandable daily education for the public.

Three-state mapping of compute resilience: the above switching directly acts on the three-tier compute network — in normal state, tasks are freely scheduled by "nearby light compute + regional heavy compute"; in load-limit state, the Origin's nearby light compute sheds load and non-critical training/inference relocates to Zhongzhiyuan and the periphery, ensuring core R&D and urban public-service continuity; in emergency state, key business is taken over across tiers and off-site data copies activated, upgrading compute from "single-computer-room disaster recovery" to "network-wide schedulable disaster recovery".

4.5 Living-Heritage Development Strategy

4.5.1 Strategy Positioning: Heritage Is the Innovation Belt's Soft Operating System

The Jingzhang Railway is the first state-owned trunk railway independently designed and built by the Chinese nation; its spirit of "self-reliance, daring to be first, and striving for excellence" is highly isomorphic with the demand for original innovation in the AI era. The 9-km linear corridor of the Jingzhang Railway Innovation Belt is both a physical archive of industrial heritage and a spatial witness of Zhongguancun moving from the "railway era" to the "intelligent era".

This strategy does not start a separate "cultural urban scenario", but understands heritage continuity as a soft operating system running through 4.1–4.4: it does not vie for dominant space for itself, but enhances the innovation belt's sense of belonging, recognizability, and global reach by providing historical narrative, place identity, and public-participation mechanisms. In other words, what 4.5 answers is "why this innovation belt deserves to be remembered and to make people want to stay", not "how large a cultural park to build".

4.5.2 Spatial Structure: One Axis, Three Belts, Five Acts, Multi-Node

Taking the old Jingzhang Railway line as the spatial skeleton, form a north–south through-connecting cultural-narrative main axis, unfolding three functional belts, five thematic acts, and multiple enterable nodes along both sides, making the 9-km corridor a continuous cultural-experience system.

4.5.3 Spatial Realization of the Three Heritage Layers

Convert heritage continuity from an abstract concept into an operable "conservation—interpretation—regeneration" three-layer structure, corresponding respectively to heritage base, narrative experience, and contemporary function.

  • Cultural roots: identification and static conservation
    Implement graded conservation for heritage elements such as station houses, tracks, bridges, crossings, signals, and industrial structures: Grade-1 objects are preserved in situ with repair and reinforcement; Grade-2 objects can be adaptively reused while retaining recognizability; Grade-3 objects are displayed through ground markers, partial restoration, and digital archives. Simultaneously establish monitoring archives for structural safety, settlement, weathering, and foot-traffic load, with data converging into the Jingzhang Culture digital platform.

  • Cultural interpretation: narrative and scene reconstruction
    Through temporal layering (e.g., Qinghuayuan Station's "three stations on one screen"), sound narrative (sound museum), symbol translation (herringbone alignment, Suzhou numerals, and sleeper-rail texture abstracted into paving/furniture/signage), AR/VR historical reconstruction, and nighttime light-and-shadow installations, let historical information be understood and felt in contemporary language.

  • Cultural regeneration: activation and participatory renewal
    Let historical space carry contemporary functions such as open-source dialogue, enterprise roadshows, community activities, study-tour education, and cultural-creative incubation. Key carriers include: the museum cluster centered on Qinghuayuan Station, the open-source trail connecting universities and communities, the rail theater retaining industrial trusses, study-tour classrooms for primary and secondary students, and community memory gardens designed with resident participation.

4.5.4 Core Strategy 1: Railway-Heritage Linear Park and Culture Trail

Taking the old Jingzhang Railway line as the spatial skeleton, build a north–south through-connecting, clearly hierarchical, narratively continuous linear cultural park and slow-traffic trail system. Three expression modes of the spatial skeleton

  • Vertical-continuation segment: in areas that cannot be expressed at ground level, such as under the Line 13 viaduct and the Jingzhang HSR entry segment, use railway-symbol spraying on pier facades, soundscape installations, light-shadow projection, and vertical greening to extend the axis memory into the air and night.

4.5.5 Core Strategy 2: Converting Industrial Relics into Creative Cultural Space

Renovation principles and spatial strategies

5.1 Planning Approval — AI-Assisted Planning-Compliance Governance

5.1.1 Scenario proposition: how dynamic planning deliverables are received by statutory process

The core contradiction of traditional planning approval is the mismatch between deliverable form and review capability: proposals move from static drawings to dynamic twins and multi-scenario simulation, yet review still relies on manual item-by-item checking of provisions, which is both slow and hard to cover all spatial rules, and the public finds it even harder to understand "why a proposal was approved and where it got stuck".

This module proposes a more fundamental judgment: planning approval should not be the terminal gate of planning deliverables, but the institutionalized interface for dynamic planning capability to enter the statutory system. It does not replace statutory review, but converts the multi-scenario proposals produced by "human–machine collaborative decision" (5.3.3) into reviewable, comparable, traceable, traceable review-decision support — letting planners shift their energy from "flipping through provisions" to "judging value".

5.1.2 Jingzhang context: why innovation-belt renewal needs compliance governance more

  • Complex tenure: Heritage Park Phase 1 involves about 13 hectares of railway-tenure land, used free of charge for public-space construction after a 2021 authorization agreement between China Railway Beijing Group and Haidian District — multi-entity authorized renewal inherently requires transparent, traceable compliance processes;
  • Multiple-plan overlap: municipal and district territorial-spatial plans overlay rail, heritage-protection (Qinghuayuan station house and other railway heritage), water-affairs (Qinghe, Xiaoyue River, Zhuan River), and transport special constraints, making manual checking highly prone to missing intersecting rules;
  • High public attention: the tension between railway-heritage conservation and urban renewal is large, and the public has strong demands for transparency (connecting to 5.4);
  • Competition context: this proposal uses provisional boundaries extensively, so compliance review naturally takes on the gatekeeping role of "distinguishing official constraints from provisional estimates and preventing provisional geometry from being mistaken for statutory red lines".

5.3 Urban Operations — Resilient Urban-Operation Monitoring and Scheduling

5.3.1 Scenario proposition: the competitiveness of urban operations is "adaptive capacity"

Traditional urban operations are divided by department, making data hard to integrate and coordination hard to process quickly. This module proposes: urban operations should not stop at "sense—dispose", but upgrade to "predict—prevent—self-adapt" — when extreme weather, large crowds, or compute shocks arrive, whether the city can preview beforehand, reorganize quickly mid-event, and auto-review afterward determines whether the Jingzhang innovation belt can fulfill its "resilient corridor" promise. This module is the operation-layer extension of 4.4 "flexible resilient computing".

5.3.2 Jingzhang context: dual rail, compute, and water systems jointly form operational complexity
